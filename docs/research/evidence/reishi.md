# Reishi (*Ganoderma lingzhi* / *Ganoderma lucidum*) — Evidence Matrix

**Product context:** Just Mushrooms ~30% ethanol fruit-body tincture, sold in South Africa. Complementary medicines are a regulated category (Category D) under the Medicines and Related Substances Act 101 of 1965, a framework established by the November 2013 amendments to the General Regulations [21].

**Research-method note.** Compiled 2026-09-02. Sources were retrieved server-side via NCBI E-utilities (PubMed abstracts), NCBI Bookshelf, GBIF's taxonomy API and MSK's About Herbs monograph. Every statement below carries a URL that was actually retrieved in this session. Anything not verified is marked **UNVERIFIED**.

**Overall grade:** **Limited human evidence** (immune markers, fatigue/quality of life as an adjunct), with **Traditional use** dominating. No trial has ever tested an ethanol fruit-body tincture.

### The taxonomic problem — and what to publish

"Reishi" is not one settled name. Historically, East Asian cultivated lingzhi was called *Ganoderma lucidum*, but *G. lucidum* in the strict sense is a European species. In 2012 Cao, Wu & Dai described the East Asian cultivated fungus as a separate species, ***Ganoderma lingzhi*** (published in *Fungal Diversity* 56(1): 54) [17]. A competing view holds that an older name has priority: Du et al. (2023) re-examined the holotype of *G. sichuanense*, recovered ITS sequences from it and from the first cultivated Chinese lingzhi specimen, and concluded that "the correct name for widely cultivated Ganoderma species in China was confirmed as *G. sichuanense*, and *G. lingzhi* should be a later synonym" [18]. GBIF's backbone follows that treatment — it lists *Ganoderma lingzhi* Sheng H.Wu, Y.Cao & Y.C.Dai as a **synonym** of *Ganoderma sichuanense* — while other GBIF-indexed checklists still carry *G. lingzhi* as an accepted species [17]. Chemistry supports the split being real: metabolomic profiling of four *Ganoderma* species found 575–764 significantly different metabolites between species pairs, with *G. lingzhi* distinguished by amino acids/derivatives and terpenes [20], and new lanostane triterpenoids continue to be described specifically from *G. lingzhi* fruit bodies [19].

**Recommendation for label and web copy:** publish **"Reishi (*Ganoderma lucidum*)"** as the primary name, because that is the name used by regulators, by the clinical literature (both Cochrane reviews [2][4]) and by MSK [1], and it is what a pharmacist or doctor will recognise. Add one clarifying line: *"Commercially cultivated reishi is now often treated as a separate species, *Ganoderma lingzhi* (some authors: *G. sichuanense*); the older name *G. lucidum* remains in general use."* Do not publish *G. lingzhi* alone as the headline name, and do not claim to know which species your raw material is unless a supplier ITS sequence says so — that is currently **UNVERIFIED** for this product.

## 2. Traditional and culinary uses

Lingzhi has been recognised as a medicinal mushroom for over 2,000 years, documented in ancient scripts, and images of it proliferate in Chinese art from about 1400 AD in association with Taoism [16]. In Chinese the name combines "spiritual potency" and "essence of immortality" [16]. The *State Pharmacopoeia of the People's Republic of China* (2000) records that it "acts to replenish Qi, ease the mind, and relieve cough and asthma," and recommends it for dizziness, insomnia, palpitation and shortness of breath [16] — the traditional basis for modern sleep/calm positioning. It has been used as an immunostimulant by patients with cancer and HIV/AIDS [1]. It is not eaten as food; commercial forms are powders, teas, spore preparations and extracts made from mycelium, spores or fruit body [16].

## 3. Compounds

| Class | Notes | Fruit body vs mycelium | Solvent behaviour | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Triterpenoids** (ganoderic acids, lanostane-type, incl. newly described ganoderanes) | Give the mushroom its **bitter taste**; content differs by mushroom part and growth stage, and the triterpene profile can be used to distinguish the fungus | Concentrated in fruit body; profile differs by species | Extracted with **ethanol** (or hot water); poorly water-soluble | [16][19] |
| **β-glucans / polysaccharides** (GL-PS, ganoderans) | Main immunomodulatory macromolecules; glucose-dominant heteropolymers with 1→3, 1→4, 1→6 β- and α-linkages | Both; submerged-culture mycelium is a major commercial source | "Normally obtained… by extraction with **hot water followed by precipitation with ethanol or methanol**" — i.e. high-molecular-weight glucans come out in water and *drop out* of concentrated alcohol | [16] |
| Peptidoglycans / proteoglycans (GLPG, GLIS, GL-PP, F3) | Water-soluble glycopeptide fractions from aqueous fruit-body extracts | Fruit body | Water | [16] |
| Spore lipids (long-chain fatty acids) | Spore-specific; spore products require wall-breaking, a costly extra step of contested benefit | Spores only | — | [16] |

**What this means for a 30% ethanol tincture.** A 30% ethanol menstruum is 70% water, so it is a *mixed* extract: it should carry the ethanol-soluble triterpene/ganoderic-acid fraction well, and will carry some water-soluble material, but it is not the hot-water process used to isolate high-molecular-weight β-glucans, and 30% ethanol is a weak solvent for triterpenes compared with the 70–95% ethanol or supercritical CO2 used industrially [16]. **The actual β-glucan and triterpene content of this specific tincture is UNVERIFIED and should be assayed before any quantitative claim ("X mg β-glucans per serving") is made.**

**2023–2024 sleep RCTs:** a targeted PubMed search for randomised trials of *G. lucidum* with sleep/insomnia/fatigue outcomes returned 10 records; **no 2023–2024 RCT with sleep as a measured outcome was found**. The nearest recent items are a 2024 fatigue trial *protocol* [10] and a 2025 Mendelian-randomisation/network-pharmacology modelling paper that merely lists *G. lucidum* among computationally predicted herbs for insomnia — not a clinical trial [22]. **Grade for sleep specifically: Unverified — do not publish.**

**Critically:** every trial above used powders, spore powders or standardised water/polysaccharide extracts (e.g. Ganopoly). **No RCT of a ~30% ethanol fruit-body tincture was found. Evidence above cannot be transferred to this dosage form.**

## 5. Animal / in-vitro summary

Preclinical work reports immunomodulatory, renoprotective, anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective effects [1]. Mechanistically, β-glucans show antitumour and immunostimulating activity; triterpenes may inhibit tumour invasion by reducing matrix metalloproteinase expression and limit metastatic attachment to endothelial cells; reishi induces NK-cell cytotoxicity via NKG2D/NCR and MAPK signalling; polysaccharides increase MHC class I expression on melanoma cells [1]. Ganoderans A and B lowered plasma glucose by up to 50% in mice at 100 mg/kg i.p. [16]. **Grade: Animal/in-vitro evidence.** None of this is a human claim.

## 6. What reishi MAY support — tiered

**Tier 1 — Limited human evidence (may be discussed cautiously, with hedging):**
- Sense of fatigue and general well-being, in people with neurasthenia [5] and in breast-cancer patients with treatment-related fatigue [6].
- Immune-cell markers (CD3/CD4/CD8 percentages) when used alongside conventional cancer care [2][11].
- Self-reported quality of life as an adjunct in cancer care [2].

**Tier 2 — Animal/in-vitro evidence only (do not present as a benefit to people):** antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, antitumour, hypoglycaemic mechanisms [1][16].

**Tier 3 — Traditional use only (must be labelled as such):** calm mind, sleep, "replenishing Qi", longevity [16].

## 7. What reishi does NOT do

- **It is not a cancer treatment.** Cochrane: "Our review did not find sufficient evidence to justify the use of G. lucidum as a first-line treatment for cancer," and it remains uncertain whether it prolongs survival [2].
- **It is not a substitute for chemotherapy, radiotherapy or any prescribed medicine.** The Cochrane signal exists only for reishi given *alongside* conventional treatment [2].
- **It does not lower blood pressure, blood glucose, cholesterol or LDL as a claim.** A Cochrane review of 398 participants found no statistically or clinically significant effect on HbA1c, lipids, BMI or blood pressure [4].
- **It does not "boost immunity."** Measured changes are small percentage shifts in lymphocyte subsets in cancer patients [2]; there is no evidence it prevents infection or illness.
- **It does not reliably improve symptoms in every fatigue population** — in Gulf War Illness the higher dose made symptoms significantly worse [9].
- It is **not proven for sleep** in any modern RCT located here.

## 8. Safety

- **Hepatotoxicity — real, documented, and the single most important caution for reishi.** MSK lists liver injury among side effects [1]. Case reports: a 2023 *Cureus* report of acute liver injury with significant transaminitis in a 47-year-old man after consuming *Ganoderma lingzhi* powder with alcohol, resolving in two weeks [12]; **fatal fulminant hepatitis** associated with lingzhi mushroom **powder** in Thailand, in a patient who had previously taken traditionally boiled lingzhi without toxicity [13]; hepatotoxicity due to a *G. lucidum* formulation reported in *J Hepatol* from Hong Kong in 2004 [14]; and a case of severe hepatitis histologically resembling autoimmune hepatitis in a patient taking *Agaricus blazei* plus *G. lucidum* extract, assessed as drug-induced liver injury by RUCAM [15]. The pattern in [13] — powdered/extracted products implicated where traditional decoctions were not — is directly relevant to a concentrated tincture. Ethanol co-ingestion is a documented aggravating factor [12].
- **Bleeding risk.** Reishi can increase the risk of bleeding with anticoagulants/antiplatelets, and reishi extracts may prolong INR, PT and APTT [1]. Warn people on warfarin explicitly [1].
- **Immunosuppressants.** Reishi can enhance immune response and may oppose immunosuppressant therapy — contraindicated without medical supervision in transplant and autoimmune patients [1].
- **Chemotherapy.** Reishi can increase plasma antioxidant capacity and may in theory interact with chemotherapeutic agents that rely on free radicals [1].
- **CYP450.** In vitro, reishi polysaccharides inhibited CYP2E1, CYP1A2 and CYP3A; clinical relevance unknown [1].
- **Antidiabetic / antihypertensive medicines.** Although the Cochrane review found no group-level glucose or blood-pressure effect [4], individual hypoglycaemic activity is reported preclinically [16]; advise monitoring rather than dose changes.
- **Laboratory interference.** Reishi **spore powder** elevates the tumour marker CA72-4, which can confound cancer monitoring [1].
- **Common side effects:** nausea, insomnia [1]; adverse events in the Cochrane CVD review were more frequent than placebo (RR 1.67) though not serious [4].
- **Allergy:** hypersensitivity to fungi/mushrooms is a general contraindication; species-specific reishi allergy data **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** no safety data located — do not use.
- **Ethanol caveats:** the product is ~30% ethanol. It is unsuitable for children, in pregnancy, for people in recovery from alcohol use disorder, for anyone taking metronidazole or disulfiram, and for people with liver disease — the latter compounded by reishi's own hepatotoxicity signal [12][13].

## 10. Disallowed claim language

- ❌ "Fights cancer" / "anti-tumour" / "shrinks tumours" / "cancer-fighting mushroom" — contradicted by Cochrane [2].
- ❌ "Boosts your immune system" / "supercharges immunity."
- ❌ "Lowers blood pressure / cholesterol / blood sugar" — contradicted by Cochrane [4].
- ❌ "Detoxifies the liver" / "protects the liver" — the human case-report literature runs the other way [12][13][14][15].
- ❌ "Clinically proven for sleep" / "natural sleeping aid."
- ❌ "Safe for everyone" / "no side effects" / "safe in pregnancy."
- ❌ Any implication it can replace or reduce prescribed medication.
- ❌ Quantitative potency claims ("10:1 extract", "30% β-glucans") until assayed — currently UNVERIFIED.

## 11. FAQ

1. **Is reishi the same as lingzhi?** Yes — lingzhi is the Chinese name; reishi is Japanese. The cultivated East Asian fungus is now often treated as its own species, *G. lingzhi* (2012), with some authors arguing the correct name is *G. sichuanense*; *G. lucidum* remains the name in common and clinical use [17][18].
2. **Does reishi treat cancer?** No. The Cochrane review of five RCTs concluded there is not sufficient evidence to justify reishi as a first-line cancer treatment, and it should never replace conventional treatment [2].
3. **Can I take it with chemotherapy?** Only with your oncologist's knowledge. Reishi may interact with chemotherapy agents that depend on free radicals, and spore products can distort the CA72-4 tumour marker [1].
4. **Will it lower my blood pressure or blood sugar?** Not as a claim — a Cochrane review across 398 participants found no significant effect on blood pressure, HbA1c or lipids [4].
5. **Is reishi hard on the liver?** Rarely, yes. Published cases include acute liver injury and one fatal fulminant hepatitis linked to concentrated lingzhi products [12][13][14]. Stop and seek medical help if you develop nausea, dark urine or yellowing of the eyes or skin.
6. **Why a tincture rather than a hot-water extract?** Ethanol pulls out the bitter triterpene fraction, while the large β-glucans are traditionally obtained by hot-water extraction and precipitate in alcohol [16]. A 30% ethanol tincture is a mixed extract, and its measured content should be confirmed by assay before any potency claim.

## 13. Verified fun facts

1. Wild lingzhi was so rare before cultivation that only the nobility could afford it, and legend placed the "sacred fungus" in the home of the immortals on the "three isles of the blest" off the coast of China [16].
2. Reishi's bitterness is chemistry you can taste: the triterpenes responsible for the bitter flavour are the same class of compounds whose profile can be used to tell the fungus apart from look-alikes [16].
3. The mushroom the world calls *Ganoderma lucidum* only received its own East Asian species name in 2012 — *Ganoderma lingzhi* — and mycologists are still arguing about it, with a 2023 study of the original 1970s herbarium specimen concluding the right name is actually *G. sichuanense* [17][18].
