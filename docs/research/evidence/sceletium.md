# Sceletium (Sceletium tortuosum, kanna) — Evidence Matrix

**Product context:** Just Mushrooms "Sceletium tortuosum 'The Happy Place'" 50 ml, 30% ethanol tincture. **Sceletium is a succulent PLANT (family Aizoaceae), not a mushroom** [1][2]; it is the only non-fungal product in the range and the only one with a psychoactive alkaloid profile, so it carries the range's highest regulatory and safety load. Sold in South Africa under the Medicines Act complementary-medicine (Category D) framework administered by SAHPRA [24][25].

**Research-method note.** Compiled 2026-09-02. Primary sources were reached through a server-side fetch tool; PubMed abstracts, the Nature abstract of Terburg 2013, the SANBI Red List, SAHPRA's Consolidated Schedules (Aug 2025, full text extracted and searched) and the Alzheimer's Drug Discovery Foundation's Cognitive Vitality report (Jan 2026) were read in full or in relevant part. Items drawn from search snippets rather than the full text are flagged "(search summary — verify)". Items that could not be verified are marked **UNVERIFIED**.

**Overall grade:** **Limited human evidence** (small, short, healthy-volunteer RCTs of one standardised extract, Zembrin) with **Traditional use**. No trial exists in people with diagnosed anxiety or depression, and none used a tincture.

**Plant vs mushroom.** Everything else in the range is a fungus; Sceletium is a flowering succulent. Website copy, category navigation and the "fruiting body / mycelium" language used for mushrooms must not be applied to this product.

## 2. Traditional and culinary uses

- Used for centuries — probably millennia — by San and Khoikhoi (Khoe-San) peoples as a masticatory and traditional medicine: chewed fresh or as fermented "kougoed", taken as tea or tincture, occasionally smoked, for thirst, hunger, fatigue, and for social and spiritual purposes [3][4][5]. Wikipedia (tertiary) notes kanna, with dagga, was an important element of the precolonial southern African exchange economy [5].
- Later adopted by European colonial farmers as a psychotropic **in tincture form** — the Terburg 2013 abstract states this explicitly [3]. This is the historical precedent for a tincture format, but it is **Traditional use**, not efficacy evidence.
- Smith et al. (1996) reviewed roughly 300 years of written records, including the original folk methods of preparing kougoed [2][6].
- SANBI records local harvesting to relieve abdominal pain and hunger and as a sedative [1].
- Not a culinary plant; no food use tradition was identified.

## 3. Compounds reported

| Compound class | Notes | Extraction relevance | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mesembrine-type alkaloids**: mesembrine, mesembrenone, mesembrenol, mesembranol | Predominant of >25 alkaloids in four structural classes (mesembrine, Sceletium A4, joubertiamine, tortuosamine). Total alkaloids ~1–1.5% (Wikipedia), but highly variable (0.05–3% in outliers per Brendler 2021 via [8]); ~0.3% mesembrine in roots, ~0.86% in aerial parts (search summary — verify) | Alkaloids are ethanol/water soluble; a 30% ethanol tincture will extract them, but **alkaloid content of the client's tincture is UNVERIFIED and should be assayed** — chemotype variation is large | [4][5][7][8] |
| Zembrin® reference | 2:1 dry extract standardised to 0.4% w/w total of the four alkaloids; 25 mg ≈ 50 mg dry plant ≈ 100–200 µg total alkaloids | Useful benchmark for comparing a tincture dose | [8][9][10] |

**Mechanism (in vitro).** A standardised ethanolic extract inhibited the serotonin transporter (SERT) with IC50 4.3 µg/ml and PDE4 with IC50 8.5 µg/ml; mesembrenone inhibited PDE4 at IC50 <1 µM (Harvey et al. 2011, reported in [5][10]). A high-mesembrine extract (Trimesemine) behaved as a monoamine-releasing agent via VMAT2 up-regulation rather than a pure serotonin-reuptake inhibitor [5][11]. Reviews also list AChE, MAO-A, CB1 and CYP17A1 inhibition and noradrenaline/dopamine transporter effects [4][5][7]. **Grade: Animal/in-vitro evidence** — mechanism, not proof of effect.

## 4. Human evidence

Every controlled trial identified used **Zembrin®, a standardised, cultivated, single-chemotype dry extract in capsules** — not a tincture and not wild-harvested plant. Results cannot be transferred to "The Happy Place" without an alkaloid assay and dose equivalence.

**Summary.** Four small placebo-controlled trials (total <120 people), all healthy volunteers, all one proprietary extract, the longest 3 months, largest n=60. ClinicalTrials.gov shows no ongoing trials (per [8]). **Grade: Limited human evidence** for short-term reduction of laboratory-induced anxiety and possible cognitive effects in healthy people; the ADDF review concludes "no clinical trials have established long-term safety beyond 3 months" and "no studies in specific diseases" [8].

## 5. Animal / in-vitro evidence summary

- Anxiolytic-like effects at low dose (5 mg/kg/day) but not high dose in restraint-stressed rats; the same study reported inflammatory-marker changes suggesting intolerance at the doses used [14][18].
- Zembrin and mesembrine reversed neuro-inflammatory and behavioural changes in rats under chronic mild stress (Gericke et al., cited in [19]); alkaloids modified anxiety-like behaviour in zebrafish [19].
- Cats given 10 mg/kg/day showed reduced stress behaviour (Hirabayashi 2004, cited in [14]) — the only animal-species datum relevant to a pet product; it is a single study and **not** a basis for pet claims.
- Toxicology: 14- and 90-day rat studies with NOAELs of 5000 and 600 mg/kg/day (highest doses tested); three genotoxicity tests negative (Murbach 2014, via [8][13]).

## 6. What Sceletium MAY support (compliant phrasing)

- "Traditionally chewed by the San and Khoikhoi peoples of South Africa." — **Traditional use** [3][5]
- "A South African succulent with a long history of use as a tea and tincture." — **Traditional use** [3][7]
- "Contains naturally occurring mesembrine-type alkaloids." — compositional statement [4][5]
- "In small studies of a standardised extract, healthy volunteers reported feeling calmer under short-term stress." — **Limited human evidence**; only publishable with the qualifier that the studies used a different, standardised extract [3][14]
- "May support a sense of calm / may help you unwind" — borderline; acceptable only as a low-risk "general health enhancement" indication under SAHPRA's Category D framework and only if the client can hold substantiation for its own product (see compliance brief). **Limited human evidence**
- "Supports focus / cognition" — **Unverified — do not publish** (Reay 2020 failed to replicate Chiu 2014) [14]

## 7. What Sceletium does NOT do / cannot be claimed

- **Not an antidepressant.** No trial in depressed patients exists; the phrase "natural antidepressant" or "natural SSRI" is a disease/therapeutic claim [8].
- **Not a treatment for anxiety disorders, panic, PTSD or insomnia.** Anxiety data are acute laboratory-stress paradigms in healthy students [3][14].
- **Not a nootropic / cognitive enhancer** on current evidence [14].
- **Not a substitute for prescribed medication**, and must never be positioned as a way to come off SSRIs (see §8).
- **Not a recreational "high" or "legal euphoric."** Recreational positioning is flagged as a concern by the US DoD OPSS and would invite scheduling scrutiny in SA [20].
- **Not "clinically proven" for this product.** The RCTs are of Zembrin, a different preparation.
- **Not a mushroom** — do not describe under mushroom taxonomy or "fruiting body" copy [1].

## 8. Safety

**Serotonergic drug interactions (the key risk).** Sceletium alkaloids inhibit serotonin reuptake and promote monoamine release [5][11]. The ADDF review states there are *no published reports* of herb-drug interactions to date (citing Brendler 2021) but that, based on mechanism, Sceletium "should not be used with drugs known to alter serotonin uptake or release, including SSRIs and SNRIs", and lists PDE4 inhibitors (roflumilast) as a further mechanistic interaction [8]. WebMD advises disclosure to a prescriber before use if taking an MAOI (within the past two weeks), an SSRI or an SNRI [21]. Combining serotonergic agents risks **serotonin syndrome** (agitation, confusion, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia); this is a mechanistic warning — no Sceletium case report was located (**UNVERIFIED** as a documented event). Tramadol, triptans, lithium, dextromethorphan and St John's Wort are standard serotonergic co-risks in interaction references; no Sceletium-specific source was found for these — **mechanism-based, UNVERIFIED**; label as "other medicines or herbs that affect serotonin".

**Adverse effects reported in trials/supplement use.** Headache (commonest, also in placebo), gastrointestinal upset, fatigue, drowsiness, difficulty concentrating [12][20]. Trial AE rates were equal to or lower than placebo at 8–25 mg/day for up to 3 months [9][13].

**Driving/operating machinery.** Drowsiness and reduced concentration are reported side effects [20]; a "may cause drowsiness — do not drive until you know how it affects you" warning is prudent. No driving-performance study located — **UNVERIFIED**.

**Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** No data on effects in pregnancy or passage into breast milk [21] — advise avoidance.

**Children.** Not studied; 30% ethanol content independently excludes children.

**Recreational misuse and adulteration.** Marketed online as a "party drug", sometimes vaped or snorted; products have been found adulterated with hordenine and ephedrine [20]. Buy certified raw material; assay for alkaloid content and adulterants.

**Dose and chemotype variability.** Alkaloid content varies up to 60-fold between plants/preparations [8]; a home-style tincture cannot be assumed equivalent to 25 mg Zembrin. Label a conservative dose and state total alkaloid content if assayed.

**Ethanol.** 30% v/v ethanol: unsuitable for children, pregnant/breastfeeding women, people with alcohol-use disorder or liver disease, and with disulfiram/metronidazole. See compliance brief §(g).

**Long-term safety.** Not established beyond 3 months [8].

## 9. South African legal status

- **Scheduling.** SAHPRA's *Consolidated Schedules* (1 August 2025) were downloaded and the full text searched: **no entry for "Sceletium", "mesembrine", "kanna" or "kougoed" was found** [22]. A newer consolidation (July 2026) is listed on SAHPRA's site but was not checked [23]. On this basis Sceletium appears to be **unscheduled** in South Africa; a vendor blog's claim that SAHPRA has classed "certain Sceletium extracts as Schedule 0" could not be traced to any notice — **UNVERIFIED** [26]. No 2020s Government Gazette notice specifically scheduling Sceletium was found. Re-check the current consolidated schedule before launch and annually.
- **Medicines Act / Category D.** A Sceletium tincture sold with any wellbeing indication is a "complementary medicine" (Category D). Because Sceletium is a psychoactive herb rather than a vitamin/mineral-type substance, it will fall under the **discipline-specific** (Western Herbal) sub-category rather than the "health supplement" annexures (which list probiotics, vitamins, minerals, amino acids, oils, etc.) [24][25]; whether Sceletium appears in any SAHPRA substance list is **UNVERIFIED**. Discipline-specific products are risk-graded: "low risk" indications (general health enhancement, health maintenance, relief of minor symptoms not related to a disease) may rely on traditional evidence; anything approaching anxiety/depression is "high risk" and requires clinical evidence [24]. The mandatory unregistered-medicine disclaimer applies (see compliance brief §(a)).
- **Regulatory review.** Brendler et al. 2021 (*Curr Neuropharmacol* 19:1384-1400) compares how Sceletium fares in modern regulatory systems and notes that bioprospecting permits must be held jointly with a South African entity (search summary — verify) [7].

## 10. Biodiversity, bioprospecting and benefit-sharing

- South Africa regulates access to indigenous biological resources under **NEMBA (Act 10 of 2004)** and the **Bioprospecting, Access and Benefit-Sharing (BABS) Regulations 2008**; commercial users need a bioprospecting or biotrade permit, with benefit-sharing and/or material-transfer agreements administered through the Bioprospecting Trust Fund [27][28][29]. South Africa ratified the **Nagoya Protocol** in 2013 [29].
- **The 2008 San Council agreement.** On 21 February 2008 HG&H Pharmaceuticals signed a benefit-sharing agreement with the South African San Council — described as the first prior-informed-consent benefit-sharing agreement with an indigenous group in southern Africa — providing up-front payments over three years plus a royalty on all Sceletium income, San Council endorsement/logo on Zembrin, and benefits directed to the Paulshoek and Nourivier communities (Northern Cape) [30][31]. On 1 October 2010 HGH received the **first bioprospecting permit** issued under NEMBA/BABS, with royalties flowing to the San Council via the Bioprospecting Trust Fund [30][32]. In March 2016 South Africa issued the world's **second Internationally Recognised Certificate of Compliance** under the Nagoya Protocol, for access to Sceletium and associated traditional knowledge [29].
- **Implication for the client.** Selling a Sceletium product commercially is "biotrade" of an indigenous biological resource with associated San/Khoi traditional knowledge. The client must be able to state (i) the source (cultivated vs wild-harvested — wild harvest is discouraged; the plant is a slow-declining Least Concern species [1][30]), and (ii) whether its supplier holds a BABS permit and benefit-sharing arrangement, or whether the client itself requires a biotrade permit from DFFE [27]. Marketing that invokes Khoisan heritage without a benefit-sharing basis is a reputational and legal risk. **Status: NEEDS CLIENT INPUT.**

2. Kanna has been chewed and brewed by the San and Khoikhoi peoples for centuries and was later taken as a tincture by Cape farmers.
3. It contains naturally occurring mesembrine-type alkaloids.
4. In small short-term studies of a standardised kanna extract, healthy adults reported feeling calmer under stress; these studies did not use a tincture and have not been repeated in people with anxiety or depression.
5. This unregistered medicine has not been evaluated by the SAHPRA for its quality, safety or intended use.
6. Do not use with antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs) or other medicines or herbs that affect serotonin. Not for use in pregnancy or breastfeeding, or by children. May cause drowsiness. Consult a healthcare practitioner before use.
7. Contains 30% alcohol by volume.
8. Our kanna is [cultivated / sourced under a BABS permit] — **insert only once verified**.

## 12. Disallowed claim language

- "Natural antidepressant", "natural SSRI", "treats anxiety/depression/PTSD", "cures stress" [8][24]
- "Clinically proven", "scientifically proven" (studies are of Zembrin, not this product) [3][9]
- "Improves memory / focus / brain power" [14]
- "Feel euphoric / get high / legal high / party" [20]
- "Safe with your medication", "no side effects", "non-habit forming" (no data)
- "Sceletium mushroom", "fruiting body" [1]
- Any use of the San Council logo or "endorsed by the San" without an agreement [30]
- "Wild-harvested Karoo kanna" as a virtue statement (sustainability and permit issue) [1][27]

## 13. FAQ

**Q1. Is kanna a mushroom?** No. It is a small succulent plant in the Aizoaceae family, related to "living stones" (Lithops), and endemic to the Cape provinces [1][4].

**Q2. Has kanna been tested in people?** Yes, but only in four small placebo-controlled studies (about 120 people in total), all healthy volunteers, all using one standardised extract (Zembrin), for at most three months [3][9][13][14].

**Q3. Will it help my anxiety or depression?** It has not been tested in people with diagnosed anxiety or depression, so no such claim can be made. Speak to your doctor; do not stop or replace prescribed medicine [8].

**Q4. Can I take it with my antidepressant?** No. Kanna affects serotonin; combining it with SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs or other serotonergic medicines carries a theoretical risk of serotonin syndrome [8][21].

**Q5. Is it legal in South Africa?** As at August 2025 Sceletium does not appear in SAHPRA's Consolidated Schedules; it is sold as an unregistered complementary medicine carrying the mandatory SAHPRA disclaimer [22][24].

**Q6. Where does the plant come from and who benefits?** Kanna is a South African endemic whose traditional knowledge belongs to the San and Khoi. Commercial use is governed by bioprospecting permits and benefit-sharing agreements; the landmark 2008 HG&H–San Council agreement channels royalties to Northern Cape communities [27][30].

## 15. Verified fun facts

1. The genus name *Sceletium* comes from the Latin *sceletus*, "skeleton", because the dried leaves keep a skeleton-like network of veins; *tortuosum* means "twisted" [2].
2. Kanna was among the first plants Linnaeus named, in 1753 — long before the Cape's Khoisan preparation "kougoed" became a subject of pharmacology [2].
3. The 2016 South African permit for Sceletium was only the second Internationally Recognised Certificate of Compliance ever issued worldwide under the Nagoya Protocol [29].
