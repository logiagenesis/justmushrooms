# Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus) — Evidence Matrix

**Product context:** Just Mushrooms ~30% ethanol fruit-body tincture, label "±1000 mg per 1 ml equivalent", sold in South Africa as a health supplement under SAHPRA jurisdiction [30][31].

**Research-method note.** Compiled 2026-09-02. Trial abstracts were read on PubMed/J-STAGE/Frontiers/PMC; full text of Saitsu 2019 and Nagano 2010 was read from the J-STAGE PDFs. Where a figure comes only from a secondary summary it is flagged "(secondary)". Anything not verified is marked **UNVERIFIED**.

**Overall grade: Limited human evidence** (small, short RCTs on cognition and mood; no large or replicated trial). Everything else is **Animal/in-vitro evidence** or **Traditional use**.

## 2. Traditional and culinary uses

- In Chinese medicine *hóu tóu gū* was traditionally used for dyspepsia, chronic gastritis, insomnia and digestive-tract complaints and is classed as a "medicinal and edible" fungus [4][16]. — **Traditional use**
- In Japan *yamabushitake* is traditionally regarded as a nerve/brain tonic; in Korea it is mainly culinary [4]. — **Traditional use**
- LiverTox (NIH) describes its historical use in Chinese medicine "generally as a tonic to relieve stress, anxiety and depression", and notes that most purported activities were shown in cells or animals, not humans [16].
- Culinary: braised, in hot pot, as a meat substitute; it is one of the few mushrooms whose fresh fruit body is eaten in large (hundreds-of-gram) portions [12].

## 3. Compounds reported

| Compound class | Where found | Extraction relevance for a 30% ethanol tincture | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hericenones** (A–I; C–H are NGF-synthesis stimulators) — aromatic resorcinol/benzyl-alcohol derivatives | **Fruit body** | Lipophilic: isolated from the *n*-hexane-soluble fraction of fruit bodies; ethanol extracts of fruit body carry hericenone B. 30% ethanol is a weak solvent for lipophilic compounds — actual hericenone yield in the product is **UNVERIFIED** (assay needed) | [17][18][21] |
| **Erinacines** (A–K; cyathane diterpenoids; NGF stimulators) | **Mycelium** (not the fruit body) | Not expected in a fruit-body-only tincture. Erinacine A crosses the blood–brain barrier in rats; hericenones have not been shown to | [9][15][17][18] |
| **β-glucans / heteropolysaccharides** (galactose, glucose, mannose, fucose) | Fruit body and mycelium | Water-extractable; standard lab method is hot-water extraction followed by *alcohol precipitation* — a crude polysaccharide fraction precipitates already at 30% ethanol (HEP-30). A hydro-ethanolic tincture therefore captures only part of the polysaccharide fraction | [19][20] |
| Ergothioneine, sterols (ergosterol), phenolic acids, lectins, fatty acids | Fruit body | Ergothioneine is water-soluble; lion's mane had the highest L-ergothioneine of seven mushrooms analysed (secondary, via LiverTox) | [16][19] |

Reviews count >200 secondary metabolites [18]; LiverTox states >1,000 components and that "which ingredient is responsible for the purported effects … [is] unknown" [16].

**Fruit body vs mycelium matters for citing trials.** Mori 2009, Nagano 2010, Saitsu 2019, Docherty 2023 and Grozier 2022 used **fruit-body powder**; Li 2020 used **erinacine-A-enriched mycelium** — its results cannot be extrapolated to a fruit-body tincture [9].

**Systematic reviews.** Menon et al. 2025 (*Front Nutr*, PROSPERO CRD42024571250) included 26 studies (5 RCTs, 3 pilot trials, 15 lab studies, 1 cohort, 1 case report) and concluded HE shows cognitive and mood benefits with "commonly unreported" side effects of stomach discomfort, headache and allergic reactions [14]. Spangenberg et al. 2025 (*Front Pharmacol*) systematically reviewed 23 **preclinical** erinacine studies only [15]. No Cochrane systematic review was identified; the trials above are indexed in Cochrane CENTRAL [29]. The Alzheimer's Drug Discovery Foundation lists the same small trials and no large trial [32].

**Summary.** Every efficacy trial is small (n = 24–77), short (4–49 weeks) and mostly Japanese/Taiwanese single-centre work; results are mixed (two null/inconclusive studies in young adults), outcome measures differ, and no trial used an ethanol tincture. The best-quality positive signal is Mori 2009 (MCI, fruit-body powder 3 g/day). **Grade: Limited human evidence.**

## 5. Animal / in-vitro evidence summary

- Hericenones (fruit body) and erinacines (mycelium) stimulate nerve-growth-factor synthesis in cultured astrocytes and in rodents; erinacine A crosses the rodent blood–brain barrier [15][17][18].
- Rodent models: reduced amyloid-β-induced memory impairment, faster recovery from ischaemic injury, antidepressant-like effects (BDNF pathway) — reviewed by Spangenberg 2025 [15][16].
- Glucose: aqueous, methanol and polysaccharide extracts lowered blood glucose and lipids in streptozotocin- or alloxan-diabetic rodents [27][28].
- Platelets: hericenone B from an **ethanol** fruit-body extract selectively inhibited collagen-induced aggregation in rabbit and human platelets in vitro [21].
- Toxicology: no genotoxicity; NOAEL 2,000 mg/kg/day for a β-glucan extract in a 90-day rat study [25]; a 2025 Frontiers Toxicology study of a mycelium/fruit-body powder found no acute, sub-chronic or genotoxic effects up to 2,000 mg/kg/day [26].
- None of the disease-model results has been replicated in a human trial.

## 6. What lion's mane MAY support (compliant phrasing)

- "A traditional culinary and tonic mushroom in China and Japan." — **Traditional use** [4][16]
- "Contains hericenones, compounds studied in the laboratory for their effect on nerve-growth-factor synthesis." — **Animal/in-vitro evidence** [17][18]
- "Small, short clinical studies have explored its effect on cognitive performance in older adults." — **Limited human evidence** [5][7]
- "Small preliminary studies have looked at mood and stress." — **Limited human evidence** [6][8][11]
- "A source of β-glucans and the antioxidant ergothioneine." — compositional statement, **Animal/in-vitro evidence** [16][19]
- Blood sugar, cholesterol, immunity, gut, nerve regeneration/repair in people — **Unverified — do not publish**

## 7. What lion's mane does NOT do / cannot be claimed

- Does **not** treat, prevent, slow or cure dementia or Alzheimer's disease. The only Alzheimer's trial was a 41-completer pilot using mycelium, not fruit body [9]; SAHPRA guideline 7.04 prohibits disease claims for health supplements [30].
- Is **not** a treatment for depression or anxiety: mood data come from 30 women eating cookies for 4 weeks [6] and one open-label obesity study [8].
- Is **not** a treatment for diabetes, cancer, ADHD, ALS or multiple sclerosis — no human trials exist (ALS: a systematic search found none [16]).
- Does **not** "regrow nerves", "boost NGF" or "increase BDNF" in humans — NGF data are cellular/animal; the one human BDNF study saw no change in BDNF [8].
- Does not deliver "guaranteed", "clinically proven" or "scientifically proven" results; the largest trial had 77 participants [8].
- Not to be equated with trial products: no trial used a 30% ethanol tincture, and the "1000 mg per 1 ml equivalent" label figure is **UNVERIFIED** — it must be substantiated by the manufacturer's extraction ratio and, ideally, an assay.

## 8. Safety

- **Adverse events in trials:** mild abdominal discomfort and diarrhoea (Mori 2009 — laboratory values normal [5][16]); abdominal discomfort, nausea, diarrhoea in ~8% over 49 weeks (Li 2020 [9]); none reported in the acute 1 g study [13]; Docherty 2023 did not report AEs [16]. LiverTox rates it "E — unlikely cause of clinically apparent liver injury" [16].
- **Allergy / hypersensitivity case reports:** occupational allergic contact dermatitis from handling fresh "White Pom Pom" (Maes et al., *Contact Dermatitis* 1999) [22]; acute respiratory distress syndrome in a 63-year-old man after 4 months of a commercial *H. erinaceum* extract, with a positive lymphocyte stimulation test (Nakatsugawa et al., *Intern Med* 2003) [23]; first reported anaphylaxis (hives, swelling, abdominal pain, diarrhoea) after eating lion's mane, skin-prick positive (Ann Allergy Asthma Immunol 2022) [24]. People with mushroom or mould allergy should avoid it.
- **Pregnancy / breastfeeding:** no human safety data located in any source consulted — **UNVERIFIED**; label should say "not recommended during pregnancy or breastfeeding unless advised by a healthcare professional".
- **Medication interactions (theoretical, from preclinical data):** antiplatelet effect of hericenone B [21] → caution with warfarin, DOACs, aspirin, clopidogrel and before surgery; glucose-lowering in diabetic rodents [27][28] → caution with insulin/antidiabetics. No human interaction study exists.
- **Ethanol-tincture caveats:** ~30% v/v ethanol is unsuitable for children, pregnancy, alcohol-dependence recovery, disulfiram/metronidazole users and some religious/dietary requirements; at 1–3 ml/day the ethanol dose is small but must be declared. Ethanol also shifts the compound profile away from the water-soluble β-glucans studied in most trials [20].
- **Quality:** commercial products vary widely; secondary sources note inconsistent processing and labelling [3]; mycelium-on-grain products differ chemically from fruit body [9][18].

## 9. Approved claim language (draft, legally cautious)

Lion's mane (*Hericium erinaceus*) is an edible mushroom with a long culinary and tonic history in China and Japan. Our tincture is made from the fruit body, the part of the mushroom that contains hericenones and β-glucans. Laboratory research has studied hericenones for their effect on nerve-growth-factor synthesis in cells and animals. A small number of short clinical studies in older adults have explored lion's mane and cognitive performance, and preliminary studies have looked at mood and stress; results are early and mixed, and larger trials are needed. This product is a health supplement, not a medicine, and is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease. Do not use if you are allergic to mushrooms, pregnant or breastfeeding, or without medical advice if you take blood-thinning or diabetes medication. Contains approximately 30% alcohol.

## 10. Disallowed claim language

- "Treats / prevents / reverses dementia, Alzheimer's, cognitive decline" — disease claim [30]
- "Cures depression / anxiety", "natural antidepressant", "for ADHD / focus disorders"
- "Regenerates nerves", "repairs myelin", "boosts NGF/BDNF levels" (human data absent or negative [8])
- "Clinically proven", "scientifically proven", "guaranteed results", "doctor recommended"
- "Lowers blood sugar / cholesterol", "fights cancer", "anti-tumour", "immune booster"
- "Same dose as used in clinical trials" (no trial used an ethanol tincture)
- Any comparison implying equivalence to a registered medicine

## 11. FAQ

**Q1. Does lion's mane improve memory?** A 2009 trial of 30 Japanese adults with mild cognitive impairment reported better cognitive-scale scores after 16 weeks of 3 g/day fruit-body powder, which faded after stopping [5]; a 2019 trial of 31 healthy over-50s saw MMSE improvement but no change on two other memory tests [7]. Studies in healthy young adults were null or mixed [10][11][12]. Evidence is limited.

**Q2. Is it safe?** Trials report mostly mild digestive upset [5][9]; LiverTox finds no liver-injury signal [16]. Rare allergic reactions — contact dermatitis, one case of severe lung inflammation and one anaphylaxis — have been published [22][23][24].

**Q3. Can I take it with blood thinners or diabetes medication?** Hericenone B inhibited platelet aggregation in the laboratory [21] and extracts lowered glucose in diabetic rodents [27][28]. No human interaction study exists, so check with a pharmacist or doctor first.

**Q4. Is it safe in pregnancy or while breastfeeding?** No safety data were found in any source [16][14]. It is not recommended.

**Q5. Fruit body or mycelium — does it matter?** Yes. Hericenones are found in the fruit body; erinacines in the mycelium [17][18]. The Alzheimer's pilot trial used erinacine-A-enriched mycelium and does not apply to fruit-body products [9].

**Q6. How much did the trials use?** 2–3.2 g/day of dried fruit-body powder for 4–16 weeks in the Japanese trials [5][6][7]; 1.8 g/day for 28 days in the UK pilot [11]; 10 g/day for 4 weeks (null) in the US exercise study [10]. Whether 1 ml of tincture is "equivalent" to 1 g of dried mushroom is **UNVERIFIED**.

## 13. Verified fun facts

1. Both halves of the Latin name mean "hedgehog": *Hericium* and *erinaceus* [3]. The Japanese name *yamabushitake* refers to the *yamabushi*, ascetic mountain monks of Shugendō, whose garments the shaggy fruit body is said to resemble [3].
2. In Chinese it is *hóu tóu gū*, "monkey-head mushroom", and a Sui-dynasty (6th–7th century) record already describes people enjoying monkey-head mushroom soup [4].
3. Lion's mane is a tooth fungus (spores borne on dangling spines rather than gills), yet DNA places it in the order Russulales alongside the gilled *Russula* and *Lactarius* [1][3].
