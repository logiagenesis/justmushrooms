# Blends & Cross-Cutting Claims — Evidence & Legality Matrix

**Scope.** This file covers the general marketing claims Just Mushrooms makes across its **blends** (e.g. Myco-Radiance, PeakO2-style stacks) and about functional-mushroom tinctures in general. Each claim gets a verdict — **Supported / Partially supported / Not supported / Prohibited** — with citations. Species-specific evidence lives in the per-mushroom files (chaga, cordyceps, shiitake, lion's-mane, reishi, tremella, turkey-tail).

**Regulatory frame.** In South Africa, these products are **Category D complementary medicines (health supplements)** under the Medicines and Related Substances Act 101 of 1965, regulated by **SAHPRA**; only **low-risk** indications and the verbs "contributes/assists/helps/aids/maintains" plus a beneficial physiological effect on generally accepted evidence are allowed, and disease claims are prohibited unless pre-approved as a disease-risk-reduction claim [1][2][3]. Advertising is additionally governed by the **Advertising Regulatory Board (ARB)** Code and SAHPRA's advertising guidance [4]. EU (EFSA), US (FDA) and UK (ASA) precedents below are not binding in SA but are the clearest published rulings on identical mushroom claims and are the benchmark a SA regulator or competitor complaint would draw on.

**Research-method note.** Compiled 2026-09-02 with server-side access to EFSA, FDA, ASA, PMC and the Merck/MSD Veterinary Manual. Search-summary figures flagged "(summary — verify)"; unconfirmed items **UNVERIFIED**.

## (a) "Stacking" / synergy of multiple mushrooms — **Not supported**

- No human trial demonstrates that combining several mushroom species produces greater or synergistic benefit than a single species. The one positive blend trial (Hirsch 2017, PeakO2, six species incl. *C. militaris*) cannot attribute its effect to any species or to "synergy" — it was a single blend vs placebo, small (n=28), and mostly driven by the *C. militaris* primary ingredient [5].
- A 2024 review explicitly frames medicinal mushrooms as **multicomponent mixtures** whose constituents "can strengthen, weaken, or complement each other" — i.e. interactions are plausible in principle but **not demonstrated to be beneficial in people**; the paper is about analytical complexity, not proof of synergy [6].
- **Verdict:** Not supported. "Synergy"/"stacking works better" is an unproven efficacy claim. Do not state or imply combined products are more effective. Compositional language ("a blend of X, Y and Z") is fine.

## (b) "Anti-inflammatory" and "anti-viral, anti-bacterial, anti-fungal" as consumer claims — **Not supported / Prohibited**

- These properties exist only in **cell and animal** studies for mushroom extracts and isolated compounds (e.g. shiitake antiviral/antibacterial preclinical work; reishi triterpenoid anti-inflammatory in vitro) [7]. No consumer-grade human evidence supports them as product benefits.
- **Legally**, "anti-viral/anti-bacterial/anti-fungal" imply treatment/prevention of infection = **disease claims**, prohibited for supplements. The FDA cited exactly this: a company's page stating cordyceps "may contain anti-viral properties… one of the most effective mushrooms for chronic viral infections" and reishi "anti-inflammatory response" was ruled to make the products **unapproved new drugs** [8].
- **Verdict:** Not supported as efficacy; **Prohibited** as worded. Avoid entirely.

## (c) "Detox" / "liver and kidney health" — **Not supported / Prohibited**

- No human evidence that mushroom tinctures "detox" or improve liver or kidney health. For chaga the only human kidney data are cases of **oxalate kidney injury** (see chaga file); for cordyceps, Cochrane found kidney evidence insufficient (see cordyceps file).
- "Detox" is a recognised **general health claim** that regulators reject: the ASA lists "detox"/"detoxifier" among general health claims that cannot stand alone [9].
- **Verdict:** Not supported; "detox" Prohibited as a standalone claim, and "liver/kidney health" unsupported and risky.

## (d) "Immune boosting" vs "immunomodulation" — **Not supported as worded; use neither for disease prevention**

The distinction matters but neither survives as an unqualified consumer claim:

- **EFSA:** rejected beta-glucan "immune defence / reduce risk of common cold" health claims — e.g. the Leiber *Yestimun* Article 13.5 dossier (2010), partly because it relied on **intravenous** beta-glucan studies deemed an "inappropriate route of exposure" [10]. EFSA has **not authorised any mushroom or beta-glucan immune claim** [10][14].
- **FDA warning letters** (mushroom-specific):
  - *Mushroom Revival, Inc.* (610361, 01 Dec 2020) — cordyceps "anti-viral… chronic viral infections" and reishi immune/cancer claims made the products unapproved new drugs [8].
  - *Entia Biosciences, Inc.* (610493, 06 Jan 2021) — lion's mane, maitake, reishi, cordyceps with "promotes lower cholesterol / balances blood sugar" etc. ruled unapproved new drugs and misbranded [11].
- **UK ASA rulings** (all Upheld, 2024):
  - *Nowt Ventures t/a Feel Güd* (A24-1239905) — "antioxidant" and "adaptogen" for cordyceps/lion's mane/chaga/reishi gummies were unauthorised specific health claims [12].
  - *Ejec Ventures t/a Auri Nutrition* (A24-1239910) — "nootropic", "adaptogen", focus/energy claims for a 12-mushroom gummy, all Upheld [13].
  - ASA also treated *Cordyceps militaris* and *Trametes versicolor* as likely **unauthorised novel foods** in the UK, breaching the Code on legality [9a].
- **South Africa:** SAHPRA allows only low-risk "helps maintain normal immune function"-type wording tied to accepted evidence and the approved verbs; "boosts immunity"/"immune booster" reads as a disease-prevention claim and is not permissible [1][2]. A specific ARB ruling on a mushroom immune claim was **not located in this session (UNVERIFIED)** — but the ARB enforces the same prohibition on unsubstantiated and medicinal claims [4].
- **Verdict:** "Immune boosting" — Not supported / Prohibited. "Immunomodulation" is a mechanism term, still unproven in humans for these products and not an authorised claim; do not use it as a consumer benefit. At most, cautious low-risk "helps support normal immune function" *may* be arguable under SAHPRA only if tied to accepted evidence — treat as **UNVERIFIED pending regulatory/legal sign-off**.

## (e) "Adaptogen" — **Not supported / Prohibited**

- **EFSA** has not authorised "adaptogen"/"adaptogenic" as a health claim; botanical "general function" claims of this type were **placed on hold and never authorised** (the ~1,548 botanical claims parked by the Commission), and EFSA's general-function assessments did not accept vague resilience/stress claims [14][15].
- **ASA** explicitly treats "adaptogen" as a **specific health claim** requiring authorisation on the GB Register; because no such authorisation exists it has been **Upheld as a breach** repeatedly (Feel Güd, Auri) [12][13].
- **Verdict:** Not supported; Prohibited as an unauthorised health claim. Do not describe any product as an "adaptogen".

## (f) Beta-glucan claims — **Partially supported (oats/barley only); mushroom beta-glucan Not supported**

- The **only authorised EU beta-glucan health claims** are for **oat and barley** beta-glucan: cholesterol lowering (Article 14 disease-risk reduction, **Commission Regulation (EU) No 1160/2011**, 3 g/day) and maintenance of normal blood cholesterol / reduced post-prandial glucose (Article 13.1, EFSA opinion 2011) [16][17][18].
- These claims are **source-specific to oats and barley** and cannot be transferred to mushroom (fungal) beta-glucans, which differ in structure (1,3/1,6-branched) and have **no authorised claim**. EFSA rejected yeast/fungal beta-glucan immune claims [10].
- Additional caveat for tinctures: even if mushroom beta-glucan had a claim, a **30% ethanol extract poorly captures high-MW beta-glucans** (see §i), so a beta-glucan benefit claim would also be compositionally weak.
- **Verdict:** Partially supported *only* for oat/barley cholesterol; **Not supported** for any mushroom beta-glucan claim. Do not imply mushroom beta-glucans carry the oat/barley cholesterol benefit.

## (g) Menopause, "leaky gut"/IBS/Crohn's, ADHD, anxiety, depression, skin-hydration — **Not supported / Prohibited**

- **Menopause:** no credible human evidence for these mushrooms; a menopause claim is a health/borderline-disease claim, not authorised — **Not supported**.
- **"Leaky gut" / IBS / Crohn's:** "leaky gut" is not an accepted diagnosis for claim purposes; IBS and Crohn's are **diseases** — any claim is **Prohibited** (disease claim). No human mushroom-tincture evidence [7].
- **ADHD:** a disease/medical condition; claims are **Prohibited**. ASA specifically flagged ADHD-treatment claims for mushroom supplements as breaches [9a].
- **Anxiety / depression:** medical conditions; treatment/relief claims are **Prohibited**. ASA upheld cases where companies could not use even substantiated-sounding stress/depression wording because it was not on the register [12][13].
- **Skin hydration:** for oral mushroom tinctures this is unproven in humans (tremella hyaluronic-acid marketing and shiitake skin data are in-vitro/topical — see tremella and shiitake files). "Skin hydration" is a specific health claim requiring substantiation/authorisation — **Not supported** for an oral product.
- **Verdict:** All Not supported; the disease ones (IBS, Crohn's, ADHD, clinical anxiety/depression) are Prohibited outright.

## (h) Pet claims — **Not supported; pet health claims separately regulated**

- The headline canine study is **Brown DC & Reetz J, 2012**, *Evid Based Complement Alternat Med* 2012:384301 — a double-blind, multi-dose pilot of **single-agent polysaccharopeptide (PSP) from *Coriolus versicolor*** (I'm-Yunity) in **naturally occurring canine haemangiosarcoma**. It reported the longest survival times then recorded for the disease, with high-dose PSP delaying metastasis [19][20].
- **Limitations (critical):** **n = 15 dogs total** across three dose arms (≈5 per arm) — a pilot, not a definitive trial; **no untreated placebo arm** (dose-comparison only); short/pilot design; **industry-adjacent** (product-branded compound); results **never confirmed** by the promised larger follow-up. It is a hypothesis-generating pilot, not proof of efficacy [19][20].
- **Legality:** animal-health claims are regulated separately — veterinary medicines/stock remedies fall under different SA legislation (Act 101 of 1965 for veterinary medicines and Act 36 of 1947 for stock remedies; **exact classification of a pet mushroom supplement is UNVERIFIED — confirm with a SA regulatory adviser**). Marketing a pet product to "treat cancer" or any disease is prohibited on the same basis as for humans.
- **Verdict:** Not supported. A single small pilot in one canine cancer does not support any pet efficacy or "cancer" claim; and see §j on ethanol toxicity — an ethanol tincture is inappropriate for pets regardless.

## (i) What ~30% ethanol extracts actually pull, and the "1000 mg/ml equivalent" figure — **Partially supported / misleading if used as a standard**

- **Triterpenes / triterpenoids (e.g. reishi, chaga betulin/betulinic acid):** ethanol-soluble — **yes**, ethanol (especially higher %) extracts these; 30% is on the low side for lipophilic triterpenoids, so yield is partial [21] and see chaga file.
- **High-molecular-weight beta-glucans / polysaccharides:** **poorly extracted by ethanol** — they are water-soluble and are **precipitated by ethanol**. This is the basis of standard **dual extraction** (hot water + ethanol) in the mushroom industry: a pure 30% ethanol tincture captures only a fraction of the beta-glucan/polysaccharide content [21][6]. So beta-glucan-dependent claims are compositionally weak for an ethanol-only tincture.
- **"1000 mg/ml dried-material equivalent" is NOT a standardised measure.** It describes a herb:extract input ratio (how much raw material went in), not the concentration of any active compound, and there is no assay behind it. It is not a pharmacopoeial or SAHPRA standard and should not be presented as a potency guarantee. Standardisation would require assaying a marker (e.g. % beta-glucan by the Megazyme method, or % cordycepin) — **UNVERIFIED whether the client does this**.
- **Verdict:** Partially supported (triterpenes yes); Not supported for beta-glucan-based claims; "1000 mg/ml equivalent" is Not a standardised potency measure and must not be implied as one.

## (j) Ethanol safety for dogs and cats — **Prohibited use; safety warning required**

- Per the **Merck/MSD Veterinary Manual**, ethanol is a recognised cause of **alcohol toxicosis** in companion animals; all species are susceptible, dogs most commonly. Signs begin ~30–60 min after ingestion: vomiting, diarrhoea, ataxia, disorientation, lethargy, tremors, dyspnoea; severe cases → metabolic acidosis, CNS depression, death [22][23].
- Cats and small dogs are especially vulnerable due to low body mass; there is **no established safe ethanol dose** for pets, and the Manual frames even bread-dough ethanol as dangerous [22][23].
- **Verdict:** A 30% ethanol tincture should **not** be given to dogs or cats. If any pet-directed product is contemplated, it must be an **alcohol-free** format, and the human tincture must carry a "keep away from pets / not for animal use" caution.

## Blend claim rules — 10 rules copywriters must follow

1. **No disease words.** Never state or imply the product treats, prevents, cures or reduces risk of any disease (cancer, diabetes, IBS, Crohn's, ADHD, anxiety, depression, infection, atherosclerosis, kidney/liver disease). Category D supplements may not carry disease claims [1][2][8].
2. **Ban the four flagged buzzwords:** "adaptogen", "detox", "immune booster/boosts immunity", and "anti-viral/anti-bacterial/anti-fungal". Each has been ruled an unauthorised or drug claim by EFSA/ASA/FDA [8][9][12][13][14].
3. **No "synergy"/"stacking works better."** Do not claim blends outperform single mushrooms; there is no human evidence [5][6].
4. **Mushroom beta-glucans carry no authorised claim.** The oat/barley cholesterol claim (Reg 1160/2011) is source-specific and must not be borrowed for mushrooms [16][17].
5. **Use only SAHPRA-style low-risk verbs** — "helps/assists/contributes to/maintains" a normal physiological function — and only when tied to generally accepted evidence for that specific ingredient; otherwise say nothing [1][2].
6. **Grade every claim** against the per-mushroom files using: Strong human evidence / Limited human evidence / Animal-in-vitro evidence / Traditional use / Unverified — do not publish. Anything below "Limited human evidence" cannot become a benefit claim.
7. **Separate compositional facts from benefit claims.** "Contains cordycepin / triterpenes / β-glucans" (if true and, ideally, assayed) is allowed; "…which boosts energy/immunity" is not.
8. **Do not present "1000 mg/ml equivalent" as potency or standardisation.** It is an input ratio, not an assay of actives [21].
9. **Get the species right.** Use valid binomials (e.g. *Cordyceps militaris*, never "Ophiocordyceps militaris"); confirm species before any species-specific claim, and note novel-food/legality risk for some species (e.g. *C. militaris*, *T. versicolor* flagged by the FSA/ASA) [9a].
10. **Pets and alcohol.** Never market the ethanol tincture for dogs/cats; carry a keep-away-from-pets caution; any pet product must be alcohol-free and disease-claim-free, and pet efficacy is not supported by the single n=15 canine pilot [19][22][23].
