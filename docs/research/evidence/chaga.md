# Chaga (Inonotus obliquus) — Evidence Matrix

**Product context:** Just Mushrooms 30% ethanol tincture, approx. 1000 mg/ml dried-material equivalent, made from the wild-harvested sterile conk (chaga has no commercially usable fruit body — see §1). Sold in South Africa as a complementary medicine / health supplement under SAHPRA jurisdiction [23][24].

**Research-method note.** This matrix was compiled on 2026-09-02 using web search only; the research environment blocked full-text retrieval from PubMed/PMC, MSK, Examine, Cochrane, GBIF and publisher sites. Every statement below is traceable to a source URL that appeared in search results, but figures drawn from search summaries rather than from the full text are flagged "(search summary — verify against full text)". Items that could not be verified at all are marked **UNVERIFIED**.

**Overall grade:** **Animal/in-vitro evidence** (with **Traditional use**). No randomised controlled trial in humans was identified.

**Why chaga is not a "fruiting body".** The black, charcoal-like mass sold as chaga is a **sterile conk (sclerotium)** — a compacted mass of mycelium and host wood, not a spore-bearing fruit body. Its colour comes from very high levels of 1,8-dihydroxynaphthalene (DHN)-type melanin [6]. The true fertile fruit body is a thin, short-lived resupinate layer that forms under the bark, typically only after the host tree dies [1][7]. Any label copy that says "chaga fruiting body" is therefore technically inaccurate; the correct description is "sterile conk" or "sclerotium". Cultivated mycelium is chemically different from the wild conk — notably it lacks the birch-derived triterpenoid profile — and polysaccharide fractions from mycelium differ from those of the conk [8].

**Sustainability / wild-harvest concern.** Commercial chaga is almost entirely wild-harvested [6]. United Plant Savers notes that a large harvestable conk represents 10–20+ years of growth and that demand has driven over-harvesting in Northern Europe, Russia and Canada; they recommend leaving at least 20% of each conk and harvesting only from living trees [7]. The often-quoted "1 in 20,000 birch trees" figure appears only in vendor material and is **UNVERIFIED**. The client should be able to state the origin country and harvest practice of its raw material.

**Edible / culinary history.** Chaga is not eaten as a mushroom. It is traditionally simmered or steeped as a dark tea, sometimes as a coffee/tea substitute in rural Russia [3][9][10].

## 2. Traditional and culinary uses

- Used as a folk remedy in Russia and other northern European countries for a range of ailments, including cancer, gastritis and ulcers [3][11].
- Kikuchi et al. record its use "in folk and botanical medicine as a remedy for cancer, gastritis, ulcers, and tuberculosis of the bones" [11].
- Chaga tea features in Solzhenitsyn's 1966 novel *Cancer Ward*, in which a rural doctor observes that peasants who drink chaga instead of tea appear not to get cancer — a literary anecdote, not evidence, but the main route by which chaga entered Western awareness [9][10][12].
- Claims that chaga was an "approved anti-cancer drug in the USSR from 1955" (the "Befungin" preparation) and "used since the 16th century" appear only in secondary/wellness sources in this search — **UNVERIFIED**; do not publish without a primary source.
- Traditional use in Indigenous North American (e.g. Cree) fire-carrying and medicinal practice is reported in historical commentary [10] — treat as **Traditional use**, not evidence.

Reported oxalate figures (search summaries — verify against full text): approx. 4,870 mg/kg dry matter (Russian material) and 6,250 mg/kg (Finnish material), "six to 100 times more than in typical edible mushrooms" [15]; other analyses report a much wider range [16]. Kwon et al. state plainly that "Chaga mushrooms contain extremely high oxalate concentrations" [14].

## 4. Human evidence

**Randomised controlled trials in humans: none identified.** Searches of PubMed/PMC-indexed reviews (2018–2025) returned only in-vitro, animal and review articles [5][17][18][19][20]. A 2024 review covering "clinical trials" of three medicinal mushrooms including *I. obliquus* exists [21], but its full text could not be retrieved; whether it contains any controlled human trial of chaga is **UNVERIFIED**. An older source cited in search results states that "to date, no human experiments have been performed" [21]. Soviet-era clinical reports are frequently mentioned in secondary literature but no citable primary source was located — **UNVERIFIED**.

**Summary.** The only human-level data for chaga are adverse-event case reports. Efficacy evidence is limited to cell and animal studies. **Grade: Animal/in-vitro evidence** (Traditional use for the tea).

## 5. Animal / in-vitro evidence summary

- Reviews (2023–2025) summarise anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-tumour, glucose-lowering, anti-obesity, hepato-/reno-protective, antibacterial and antiviral activity **in cells and rodents** [5][18][19].
- Chaga triterpenoids inhibited dihydrofolate reductase and were cytotoxic to breast-cancer cell lines in vitro [17]; extracts from conks on *Betula pendula/pubescens* were cytotoxic to several cancer lines in vitro [13]; ergosterol peroxide down-regulated β-catenin signalling in colorectal cancer cells [3].
- Extracts modulated macrophage polarisation in vitro [25]; polysaccharides were tested in zebrafish [26]; a 2025 mouse study reported reduced folic-acid-induced renal fibrosis [27].
- Betulinic acid showed anti-obesity effects in high-fat-diet mice [6].
- None of these findings has been reproduced in a human trial.

## 6. What chaga MAY support (compliant phrasing)

- "Traditionally brewed as a tea in Russia and Northern Europe." — **Traditional use** [3][11]
- "A natural source of antioxidant polyphenols and melanin." — **Animal/in-vitro evidence** [5][6]
- "Contains birch-derived triterpenoids such as betulin and betulinic acid." — compositional statement, **Animal/in-vitro evidence** [6]
- "Being studied in laboratory research for its effects on immune cells." — **Animal/in-vitro evidence** [25]
- Any statement about blood sugar, inflammation, immunity or cancer in people — **Unverified — do not publish**

## 7. What chaga does NOT do / cannot be claimed

- Does **not** treat, prevent or cure cancer. No human trial exists; MSK lists cancer only as a folk use [3]. Disease claims are prohibited for health supplements under SAHPRA guideline 7.04 [23][24].
- Does **not** "detox", "cleanse the kidneys" or "support kidney health" — the only human kidney data are cases of kidney injury [11][14].
- Does **not** have evidence for blood-sugar control, immunity, energy, longevity or "adaptogen" effects in humans [5][19].
- Cannot be described as a "fruiting body" product (see §1) [6][7].
- Cannot be marketed as a food in the EU other than in limited supplement forms: the European Commission terminated the chaga novel-food procedure in March 2022; only the conk and aqueous/ethanolic extract are regarded as non-novel **in food supplements** (search summary — verify) [28][29]. Not directly binding in SA, but a useful benchmark for export.

## 8. Safety

**Oxalate nephropathy (the key risk).** Two biopsy-confirmed case reports: Kikuchi 2014 (72-y woman, 4–5 tsp powder/day for 6 months) [11] and Kwon 2022 (69-y man, 10–15 g/day plus 500 mg vitamin C for 3 months; nephrotic syndrome and acute oxalate nephropathy) [14]; a pharmacovigilance abstract in *Reactions Weekly* 2022 [30] and a possible Korean ESRD case [22] (UNVERIFIED). Both confirmed cases involved large daily doses of whole powder; the oxalate load of a 30% ethanol tincture at label doses is **UNVERIFIED** and should be measured. Advise against use in people with kidney disease, kidney stones, or those taking high-dose vitamin C [14].

**Hypoglycaemia (theoretical).** Glucose-lowering effects in rodents [5][19] mean additive effects with antidiabetic drugs are plausible; no human cases located — **Animal/in-vitro evidence**.

**Anticoagulant interaction (theoretical).** Commonly listed in professional monographs (MSK) but the MSK text could not be retrieved in this session — **UNVERIFIED here; confirm at [3] before publishing**.

**Pregnancy / breastfeeding.** No human data located — **UNVERIFIED / no data; advise avoidance**.

**Allergy.** No published case series located — **UNVERIFIED**; standard "discontinue if reaction" advice.

**Ethanol-tincture caveats.** A 30% v/v ethanol preparation is unsuitable for children, pregnant women, people with alcohol-use disorder or liver disease, and those taking disulfiram/metronidazole; label the alcohol content. Specific SA labelling rules for alcohol in complementary medicines: **UNVERIFIED** in this session — check SAHPRA labelling guidelines [23].

## 9. Approved claim language (draft, legally cautious)

1. Just Mushrooms Chaga is a 30% ethanol extract of wild-harvested chaga conk (*Inonotus obliquus*), a fungus that grows on birch trees in northern forests.
2. Chaga has a long history of traditional use as a brewed tea in Russia and Northern Europe.
3. Chaga is a natural source of polyphenols, melanin and birch-derived triterpenoids including betulin and betulinic acid.
4. Laboratory research is exploring how chaga compounds interact with cells; these effects have not yet been confirmed in human clinical trials.
5. This product is a health supplement and is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease.
6. Chaga is naturally high in oxalates; people with kidney disease or a history of kidney stones, and anyone pregnant, breastfeeding or on prescription medicines, should consult a healthcare practitioner before use.
7. Contains 30% alcohol by volume; not for children.

## 10. Disallowed claim language

- "Anti-cancer", "fights tumours", "the king of medicinal mushrooms for cancer" [3][23]
- "Cures/prevents diabetes", "balances blood sugar" [5]
- "Detoxes/cleanses the kidneys or liver" [11][14]
- "Boosts immunity", "immune booster" (no human data) [5]
- "Clinically proven", "scientifically proven", "doctor recommended"
- "Chaga fruiting body extract" (factually wrong) [6][7]
- "Highest antioxidant food on earth / ORAC" claims (no verified source)
- "Safe for everyone", "no side effects" [11][14]

## 11. FAQ

**Q1. Is chaga a mushroom?** Not in the everyday sense. What is harvested is a sterile conk — a hardened mass of fungal tissue and birch wood — rather than a spore-producing fruit body [1][6][7].

**Q2. Has chaga been tested in people?** No randomised controlled trials in humans were identified; research so far is in cells and animals [5][19][21].

**Q3. Is chaga safe for my kidneys?** Chaga is exceptionally high in oxalates, and at least two biopsy-confirmed cases of oxalate kidney injury have been reported after months of high-dose powder use. People with kidney disease or kidney stones should avoid it [11][14][15].

**Q4. Can chaga treat cancer?** No. Cancer is a traditional folk use, not a demonstrated effect; only laboratory studies exist [3][17].

**Q5. What is in chaga?** Polysaccharides (β-glucans), polyphenols, melanin, and birch-derived triterpenoids such as betulin, betulinic acid and inotodiol [5][6].

**Q6. Is wild chaga sustainable?** Demand has driven over-harvesting; a large conk takes 10–20+ years to form. Responsible harvest leaves part of the conk and takes only from living trees [7].

## 13. Verified fun facts

1. Chaga's charcoal-black, cracked crust owes its colour to massive amounts of DHN-type melanin — the same pigment family that darkens many fungi [6].
2. The conk actually contains more betulinic acid than the bark of the birch tree it grows on [6] (search summary — verify).
3. Chaga owes much of its Western fame to a novel: Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn's *Cancer Ward* (1966), in which prisoners in a Soviet hospital discuss a rural doctor's chaga-drinking patients [9][12].
