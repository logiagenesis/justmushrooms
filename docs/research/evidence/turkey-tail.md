# Turkey Tail (*Trametes versicolor*) — Evidence Matrix

**Product context:** Just Mushrooms ~30% ethanol fruit-body tincture, sold in South Africa. Complementary medicines are a regulated category (Category D) under the Medicines and Related Substances Act 101 of 1965, per the November 2013 General Regulations amendments [17].

**Research-method note.** Compiled 2026-09-02 via NCBI E-utilities (PubMed), PubMed Central, GBIF's taxonomy API and MSK's About Herbs monograph. Every claim carries a URL retrieved in this session; unverified items are marked **UNVERIFIED**.

**Overall grade:** **Strong human evidence — but only for PSK, a licensed Japanese pharmaceutical made by hot-water extraction of cultured mycelium.** For turkey tail as a fruit-body ethanol tincture: **Limited human evidence** at best, and mostly **Traditional use**.

**Naming recommendation:** publish **"Turkey Tail (*Trametes versicolor*)"** and add "(syn. *Coriolus versicolor*)" once, because most of the clinical literature and MSK's monograph are filed under *Coriolus* [1][2]. Unlike reishi, there is no live species controversy here.

## 2. Traditional and culinary uses

Turkey tail's medicinal use within Chinese traditional medicine dates back at least 2,000 years and is associated with general health-promotion, endurance and longevity [2]. In both China and Japan the traditional preparation is a dried powdered tea of the fungus [2]. MSK records its traditional Chinese medicine use as a **tonic** [1]. It has never been a food mushroom — it is tough and leathery — so all traditional and modern use is as a decoction, powder or extract.

## 3. Compounds — and why PSK/PSP are not tinctures

| Class | Notes | Fruit body vs mycelium | Solvent behaviour | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **PSK (polysaccharide-K, Krestin)** | A **protein-bound polysaccharide** developed as a pharmaceutical in Japan from the **CM-101 strain**; used as a chemoimmunotherapy agent in Asia for over 30 years | Produced from **mycelial biomass** in submerged culture (5–7 days, 25–27 °C) | Recovered by **hot-water extraction**, then concentrated, purified and dried to a powder | [3][4][2] |
| **PSP (polysaccharopeptide)** | The Chinese counterpart, from the **COV-1 strain**; isolated from mycelium and fermentation broth | Mycelium/broth | Hot-water extraction | [2][5][3] |
| **VPS** | A further hot-water extract product sold as a supplement | — | Hot water | [1] |
| β-glucans and other polysaccharides | The main bioactive class; the fruit body is otherwise carbohydrate, protein, amino acids and minerals | Both | High-molecular-weight glucans are water-extracted, not alcohol-extracted | [2] |
| Small-molecule constituents incl. terpenoids | Present, but "not established as the main components," and their contribution to activity requires further research | Fruit body | Some ethanol-soluble; 70% ethanolic Yunzhi extracts have been used in cell work | [2] |

**The single most important compliance point on this page.** PSK and PSP are **proprietary pharmaceutical preparations** derived from two specific single fungal strains (CM-101 in Japan, COV-1 in China), produced by **hot-water extraction of submerged-culture mycelium**, purified, standardised, and **approved as medicines, primarily as adjuvants in cancer therapy** [2][3][4]. A ~30% ethanol tincture of wild-type fruit bodies is a different organism preparation, a different fraction (ethanol favours small molecules; hot water favours the protein-bound polysaccharides), a different molecule and a different dose. **PSK/PSP trial results cannot be attributed to this product, and citing them in marketing would be a misrepresentation.** MSK makes the parallel point that over-the-counter products are not standardised, making potency incomparable between brands, and that "it is also unclear if PSK, PSP, and other Coriolus extracts have comparable effects" [1]. Over 100 strains of the fungus are known, so genetic and cultivation variability is substantial [2]. The β-glucan content of this specific tincture is **UNVERIFIED** pending assay.

**Summary.** The strong evidence in this species belongs to a licensed drug used as post-surgical adjuvant immunochemotherapy in Japanese and Chinese oncology — not to a supplement, and not to this dosage form. The only trial of a plain *T. versicolor* preparation in the West is Torkelson 2012: **nine women, no control group, immune markers only, explicitly a phase I safety study** [6]. MSK's own summary notes that findings on breast cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma and leukaemia are **mixed**, and that the US FDA has not approved coriolus extracts as cancer treatments [1].

## 5. Animal / in-vitro summary

*C. versicolor* is described as a biological response modifier [1]. In vitro, PSK, PSP and coriolus extract induce apoptosis in leukaemia cells; PSP reduces proliferation and prolongs DNA synthesis time; a mushroom blend induced G2/M arrest and suppressed migration/invasion in an invasive breast cancer cell line via urokinase plasminogen activator suppression; PSP inhibits HIV-1 gp120–CD4 interaction and reverse transcriptase in vitro [1]. In animals, PSK synergises with biologic therapies, PSP induces cytokine production and T-cell proliferation, prevents cyclophosphamide-induced immune suppression, and shows analgesic activity in mice [1]. **A cautionary animal result:** high doses of a hot-water coriolus extract (VPS) *enhanced* development of large-intestinal tumours in carcinogen-treated mice — MSK judges this not clinically relevant because the dose was 10–13× human levels and the mice received potent carcinogens, but it should not be omitted from an honest file [1][18]. **Grade: Animal/in-vitro evidence.**

## 6. What turkey tail MAY support — tiered

**Tier 1 — Limited human evidence (discuss cautiously, hedged, never as treatment):**
- Immune-cell markers (lymphocyte counts, NK activity, CD8+/CD19+ subsets) in post-treatment breast cancer patients — trends only, n = 9, uncontrolled [6].
- Gut microbiome modulation as a prebiotic, in healthy adults, using PSP [11].

**Tier 2 — Strong human evidence, but NOT transferable to this product:** PSK as adjuvant immunochemotherapy after curative resection of gastric or colorectal cancer [7][8][9]. This belongs in an educational context only, always explicitly labelled as a different, pharmaceutical preparation.

**Tier 3 — Animal/in-vitro evidence only:** apoptosis induction, antimetastatic and cytokine effects [1].

**Tier 4 — Traditional use only:** tonic use in Chinese medicine; general health promotion, endurance and longevity; drunk as a powdered tea [1][2].

## 7. What turkey tail does NOT do

- **It is not a cancer treatment.** The FDA has not approved coriolus extracts as cancer treatments [1], and the survival evidence belongs to PSK given *with* chemotherapy after surgery [7][8].
- **It is not a substitute for chemotherapy, radiotherapy or surgery.** In every positive trial, PSK was **added to** conventional treatment, never used instead of it [7][8][9].
- **A tincture is not PSK or PSP.** Those are purified, standardised hot-water mycelial extracts from specific strains, approved as medicines [2][3][4]. Nothing about their trial results may be claimed for this tincture.
- **It does not "boost immunity."** The human immune data are marker trends in nine women without a control group [6].
- **It does not prevent cancer recurrence as a claim** — the 2019 meta-analysis found no significant effect of *C. versicolor* products on relapse-free survival (HR 1.19; 0.91–1.55) [10].
- **It does not treat HPV or cervical lesions** in this form — that evidence is a vaginal gel, a different product and route [1][15].
- **Potency is not comparable between brands.** Many over-the-counter products are not standardised [1].

## 8. Safety

- **Generally well tolerated.** Coriolus extracts are generally well tolerated, with minor adverse effects [1]. In the phase I trial, up to 9 g/day was safe and tolerable in women after breast-cancer treatment [6]. PSK meta-analyses found no increase in side effects [9].
- **Known adverse reactions (rare):** dark-coloured stools not originating from occult blood; darkening of the fingernails; low-grade haematological and gastrointestinal toxicity when used with chemotherapy — though these may be caused by the chemotherapy itself [1][19].
- **Immunosuppressants:** turkey tail is characterised as an immunostimulant/biological response modifier [1], so it is inappropriate without medical supervision for transplant recipients and people on immunosuppressive therapy. Specific documented interaction reports: **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Chemotherapy:** do not use during cancer treatment without the treating oncologist's knowledge [1].
- **Bleeding risk / anticoagulants:** no anticoagulant interaction is listed for coriolus in the MSK monograph [1] — unlike reishi. Do not import reishi's bleeding warning here; a specific turkey tail bleeding risk is **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Antihypertensive / antidiabetic medicines:** no documented interaction located — **UNVERIFIED**; advise medical supervision as a general precaution.
- **Allergy:** mushroom/fungal hypersensitivity is a general contraindication; species-specific allergy data **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** MSK states that until safety data become available, pregnant or breastfeeding women should avoid coriolus [1]. Do not use.
- **Ethanol caveats:** at ~30% ethanol, this product is unsuitable for children, in pregnancy or breastfeeding, for people in recovery from alcohol use disorder, for those with liver disease, and for anyone taking metronidazole or disulfiram.
- **Wild-harvest identification:** *T. versicolor* has several look-alike polypores. Species confirmation of raw material is a quality-control requirement; the identification method used for this product's raw material is **UNVERIFIED**.

## 10. Disallowed claim language

- ❌ "Clinically proven to improve cancer survival" / any use of the PSK hazard ratios as if they applied to this tincture [7][8].
- ❌ "PSK", "PSP", "Krestin" or "polysaccharide-K" anywhere on the label or product page — these name specific pharmaceutical products the company does not sell.
- ❌ "Anti-cancer", "tumour-fighting", "prevents recurrence" [1][10].
- ❌ "Boosts your immune system" / "strengthens immunity."
- ❌ "Used in hospitals in Japan" (true of PSK, false of this product).
- ❌ "Kills HPV" / "clears cervical lesions" [1][15].
- ❌ "Safe for everyone" / "safe in pregnancy" — contradicted by MSK [1].
- ❌ Quantitative β-glucan or "1:1 equivalent PSK" claims — UNVERIFIED without assay.

## 11. FAQ

1. **Is turkey tail the same as *Coriolus versicolor*?** Yes. *Coriolus versicolor* is a synonym; the accepted scientific name is *Trametes versicolor* (L.) Lloyd, and most clinical papers are still filed under *Coriolus* [2][16].
2. **Is turkey tail an approved cancer treatment?** PSK, a purified extract, is used in Japan and China as an adjuvant after primary cancer treatment, but the US FDA has not approved coriolus extracts as cancer treatments, and no mushroom supplement is a cancer treatment [1].
3. **Isn't there strong evidence it improves survival?** For PSK specifically, yes: meta-analyses of centrally randomised trials found a survival hazard ratio of 0.88 in gastric cancer (8,009 patients) and 0.71 in colorectal cancer (1,094 patients), always added to chemotherapy after surgery [7][8]. PSK is a hot-water extract of cultured mycelium of one strain, manufactured as a drug — not a fruit-body tincture [3][4].
4. **Has a turkey tail supplement been tested in people?** Only in a small way. A phase I trial gave 3–9 g/day of a *T. versicolor* preparation to nine women after breast-cancer radiotherapy for six weeks; it was safe and showed immune-marker trends, with no control group [6].
5. **Can I take it while on chemotherapy?** Only with your oncologist's knowledge. Turkey tail behaves as an immune modulator, and product potency is not standardised between brands [1].
6. **Why doesn't the tincture list a PSK content?** Because it does not contain PSK. PSK is a specific proprietary product from the CM-101 strain [2][4], and quantitative β-glucan content of this tincture has not been assayed.

## 13. Verified fun facts

1. Two of the world's best-known mushroom medicines come from two single laboratory strains of this one fungus: **PSK (Krestin)** from strain **CM-101** in Japan, and **PSP** from strain **COV-1** in China — both approved as medicines, and both made by hot-water extraction of mycelium grown in fermenters, not from wild mushrooms [2][3][4].
2. Turkey tail was one of the first mushrooms shown to behave as a **prebiotic** in a randomised human trial: PSP produced clear, consistent shifts in the gut microbiome of healthy volunteers, while amoxicillin's disruption still had not resolved 42 days after the antibiotic stopped [11].
3. Its two rarest recorded side effects are unusually cosmetic for a medicine: **darkening of the fingernails**, and dark-coloured stools that are not caused by any bleeding [1][19].
